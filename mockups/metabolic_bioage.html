The Metabolism screen shows twenty-four numbers. One of them is a measurement.

The screenshots are the base layer — every card, in the source screen's own grammar, rebuilt on the app's dark palette and the project's own _BIOAGE_COLORS["metabolism"]. What is new is the data underneath. There is a 142-scan bioimpedance export sitting unused in Input_files/ running 7 Jun 2024 → 3 Aug 2026 from a Foryond foot-only scale, and a five-scan InBody 770 print-out from the gym. Between them they settle what the screenshots could not. On the scale, the body fat percentage is itself predicted by your weight and the date, so its impedance sensor is not detectably contributing anything. On the InBody — the better instrument by far — the height was typed in wrong on four of five scans, and height is squared inside the first step of its calculation. Two numbers survive all of it, and they are the two nobody looks at.

The gym machine was run against a different height nearly every visit, and height is squared inside the first step of its calculation. The InBody 770 print-out carries a five-scan history. Back-solving √(weight ÷ BMI) on each row gives the height it was told: 185.5 · 175.1 · 174.9 · 181.6 · 181.8 cm. The true figure is 182. Only the last two scans were close.

The proof is a pair of scans eight minutes apart, and it needs no curve-fitting. On 21 May 2025 at 12:16 and 12:24 the weight was identical — 79.5 kg both times — while BMI moved 26.0 → 24.1. BMI is weight ÷ height². At an unchanged weight a moved BMI can only be a moved height. That establishes the re-type before a single composition number is looked at. What it did: body fat 20.0 → 14.0%, skeletal muscle 35.9 → 38.8 kg, total body water 46.7 → 50.2 L. The device reallocated 4.8 kg from fat to lean in eight minutes. Measured sensitivity: −0.89 pp of body fat per centimetre.

The compartments do move roughly as height² — water ×1.075 against a height-squared ratio of 1.079 — but that is corroboration, not proof, and an earlier version of this page overstated it. BMI prints to 0.1 and water to 0.1 L, so the residual between those two ratios is unresolved anywhere in [−0.96%, +0.25%], a window that straddles zero. Lean leg mass, printed to 0.01 kg, moved 3.75% more than a pure rescaling predicts. The honest statement is that the compartments tracked height² to within the precision the sheet can express — which is all the data can support, and all the argument needs.

Across those same eight minutes the phase angle held at 6.1° and the ECW/TBW ratio held at 0.377. Neither can move, because height does not appear in either — both are ratios of quantities the electrodes measured directly. That is the whole argument of this page, demonstrated by the better machine on itself.

Recompute every scan at the true 182 cm and something real comes out — this page previously wrote it off, and that was wrong. Rescaling each scan's water by (182 ÷ height entered)² and running it back through the machine's own chain does two things. First, the two scans eight minutes apart go from 6.0 pp apart to 0.31 pp apart — 13.30% and 13.62%. That agreement is the test of the correction, and it passes convincingly.

Second, the printed spread across all five scans falls from 8.2 pp to 4.64 pp. So 3.6 points were the keyboard — and 4.6 points are the body. Between 13 January and 27 June 2025, both ends corrected: −4.22 kg of fat, +0.72 kg of lean, on a weight change of −3.5 kg. The three numbers reconcile exactly. That is textbook recomposition, and it lands squarely inside the year's strongest training block — pull PRs that May, and the annual overhead-press best of 15 kg × 9 on 28 June, the day after the final scan. The left rhomboid strain came the following morning, Sunday 29 June 2025. So the last clean InBody reading catches this body at its 2025 peak, forty-eight hours before the injury that ended the block.

And it is not an artefact of choosing the square. Letting the height exponent float anywhere from 0 to 6, the minimum achievable spread is 3.81 pp at exponent 1.5. No power of height makes the signal disappear. Correcting a measurement is not the same as dismissing it — and the correction is what let the real change become visible.

The scale's body fat percentage is predicted by your weight and the date, to finer than it prints. Regressing it on those two alone across all 142 scans gives −23.68 + 0.4570 × weight + 0.1202 × age with R² = 0.9966 and a residual scatter of 0.051 pp — half the 0.1 pp step the screen displays. The tell is not the fit, it is the repeats: the scale read 78.8 kg on three occasions spread over 111 days and printed 16.0% every time, and 80.9 kg four times over 86 days, 17.0% every time. Real body fat at a fixed weight moves with hydration and glycogen by far more than nothing.

So the impedance measurement, if it happens at all, is being overwhelmed by a weight-and-age formula. That collapses the export to one free number, and it means the fat-versus-lean split on this page is a statement about weight wearing the vocabulary of body composition. It is still the best split available — but the honest reading of “fat mass rose 0.7 kg” is “weight rose, and the formula assigned some of it to fat.”

The badges are inverted: the one metric that is genuinely measured is the only below-average one, and it is the only one with no badge at all. Phase angle 6.1° comes from resistance and reactance directly, with no population model in between. Against reference percentiles for a 30-year-old male (P5 5.70 · P25 6.42 · P50 6.91) it sits around the 13th–16th percentile, and against male athletes it is at or below the 5th. It is displayed bare.

Meanwhile both red “Elevated” flags are on numbers that cannot be anything else. Fat-free mass is about 73.2% water by definition, so 69.4 kg of it obligates 50.8 L — the scan read 51.0, over by 0.39%. And the ratio that would actually indicate fluid overload, extracellular over total water, is 37.6%, inside the healthy 36–39% band and on its better half — and carries no flag. The device is flagging absolute litres against a population that is fatter than this one. Being lean is being read as being swollen.

The headline number is the least reliable thing on the screen. “Metabolic BioAge” is not a measurement and not a composite of many measurements. Fitted against the export it is −20.73 + 1.226 × body fat % + 0.900 × chronological age — R² = 0.929, and rounding that fit to an integer reproduces the printed value on 120 of 142 scans and lands within one year on 142 of 142. So it carries one physiological input, body fat percent, plus your birthday. Everything else about it is presentation.

The usual way a vendor builds one of these is to compute a resting metabolic rate and then report the age whose population-average rate matches it. That is not what happened here. Solving the three standard reference equations for the age that yields 1869 kcal at this height and weight gives 11.9 years (Mifflin-St Jeor), 25.8 (Harris-Benedict revised) and 28.1 (Harris-Benedict 1919) — a 16-year spread, and none of them is 21. The number is not reconstructible from any published method.

Two devices make the point without any modelling at all. On the same body, sixteen days apart, the InBody read 21 and the scale read 26 — five years apart, against a real age of 30.7. A number that changes by five years depending on which device you stand on, and by one year between two scans two minutes apart, cannot be the thing the page is about.

A correction, kept on the page rather than quietly edited out. An earlier version of this review led with “the two devices disagree by 4.0 pp on body fat”, drawn from a single pairing. The InBody's five-scan history shows that framing was wrong. Across all five paired dates the gap runs from −4.2 to +3.7 pp — but almost all of that spread is the height field, not the devices. Restricted to the two scans with a near-correct height entered, the InBody read 2.3 and 4.0 points leaner than the scale, a consistent direction on n=2 rather than a random disagreement.

On weight the two agree closely and always did: mean gap +0.24 kg across five pairings, and on 21 May 2025 they read 79.5 and 79.5 — identical. The devices were never the problem. One of them was misconfigured, and the other measures one thing.

What the export is genuinely good for is the trend, and the trend tells a clinical story the app does not currently know. There are zero scans between 21 Oct 2025 and 27 Jan 2026 — a 97-day hole that sits exactly on the index mid-back episode (MRI 10 Nov 2025, re-injured roughly every two weeks for six weeks). He stopped standing on the scale. He came back +4.4 kg heavier and +2.0 pp fatter, and the vendor's body age stepped 28 → 30.

Within one device the direction is trustworthy even though the absolute percentage is not, because the same estimator is making the same error every time. That is the distinction the page has to teach: compare a device against itself, never against another device.

Body analysis

Why the hero is composition, not an age

Fat mass and fat-free mass in kilograms is the most the data supports, and it is the question that actually matters during a rehab-to-strength block: the +1.4 kg since 17 July is either fat coming back or lean mass arriving, and those need different responses.

It also degrades honestly. If the scale is not used for three months the card can say “last measured 97 days ago”. An age in years has no way to admit that — it just keeps printing 21.

The measurement floor, and why it is no longer on the screen

Across 100 pairs of scans two days apart or less, weight moved a mean 0.50 kg (max 1.50) with no physiology in between. The screen prints to 0.1 kg — roughly five times finer than it can repeat.

An earlier draft printed that floor beside every change. It was the right fact in the wrong place: a single-reading noise figure does not describe the error on a trend, which is what the chart now shows. Fitted over four weeks the smallest detectable trend is 1.19 kg/month at one weigh-in a week and 0.80 at four. That is the number worth surfacing, and it belongs beside the trend line rather than beside a delta.

What a phase angle is

Bioimpedance passes a small alternating current through the body. The opposition splits in two: resistance, from the fluid, and reactance, from cell membranes — each membrane is a thin insulator between conductive fluid, which is physically a capacitor, and a capacitor makes the current lag the voltage.

The phase angle is that lag, arctan(reactance ÷ resistance). Higher means more intact, well-charged cells. It is a property of your tissue, not an amount of it — which is why it can sit still while your weight moves, and why fat barely registers in it at all: fat is nearly dry and carries little membrane per kilogram.

Height, settled — and a step to watch for

Nothing in the repo carried a height. Back-solving √(weight ÷ BMI) across all 142 scale rows gives 183.0 cm, sd 0.11 — that tight only because the device stores one constant and divides by it. The gym machine implied 181.8 cm. The true figure is 182 cm: the scale was set 1 cm high, the trainer's entries were all over the place.

The scale was corrected to 182 on 5 Aug 2026, and the re-export shows it did not back-apply — all 142 historical rows stay at 183.0. So the next weigh-in will step from the setting alone: BMI +0.27 guaranteed, and body fat +0.9 pp (17.6 → ~18.5) if this device uses height the way InBody does. The next scan is the test. That step is a setting, never fat gain — which is exactly the mistake the gym made five times, in the other direction.

The empty cards are not empty at the source

Waist, hip and waist-to-hip read as blank on the phone. The InBody print-out for the same scan carries waist 82.8 cm and waist-to-hip 0.84. The app simply did not sync them — along with the raw impedance table, the scan history and the fitness score. Keep the print-out, not the app summary.

Those two are still worth a tape, because InBody's are estimated from impedance rather than measured. A tape needs no model and no height field, and waist carries real metabolic-risk information that nothing else here does.

Badge colour is close to uncorrelated with what matters

The only genuinely below-average reading, phase angle at 6.1°, carries no badge. Both red badges are on numbers that could not have come out any other way. And the InBody's own balance check reads “Balanciert” on all three axes, so the small left-right lean difference the app shows is not flagged by the machine that measured it either.

The two devices disagree about a word, not just a number

Both print “Muscle mass”. The screenshots say 39.5 kg, the scale says 64.4 kg. Neither is wrong — the scale means fat-free mass minus bone, the screenshots mean skeletal muscle. Line the definitions up and the gap falls from 23 kg to 3 kg.

Any ingest has to map vocabularies before it maps numbers, or the first chart drawn across both devices will show a 23-kilogram overnight muscle gain.

Three cards are empty, and they are the cheapest ones to fill

Waist, hip and waist-to-hip are blank on every screenshot. They need a tape measure, not a device — and waist circumference predicts metabolic risk at least as well as anything else on this screen, without any impedance model in the way.

Visceral fat is the contrast: the screen gives it in cm² and as a level, which looks like an imaging result. It is a straight line off body fat percent — on the export, level = −2.893 + 0.593 × body fat %, worst residual 0.144.

What this does not touch

Nothing here goes near the engine. Traffic light, ACWR, readiness and the volume recommendation are unchanged, and body composition should stay out of all four until it is measured on a fixed cadence — Key Rule 2 keeps safety deterministic and this is not a safety input.

It also does not become manual biometric entry. Key Rule 4 forbids that, and a CSV export from a device is a sync, not a hand-typed number.

Phase angle is the one screenshot metric worth chasing

6.1° is the only value on either device that is close to a raw electrical measurement rather than a model output — it comes from the resistance and reactance directly. It is also the only one the scale export does not carry at all.

Which makes it the strongest argument for finding out what device produced the screenshots and whether it exports.

Two devices, one body, five paired dates

Each InBody scan matched to the nearest scale reading — four of the five land on the same day or the next. Read the weight row first: the two instruments agree throughout. Everything below it is model output, and the spread in the body-fat row is the height field, not a disagreement between the devices.

QuantityInBody 770Foryond scaleGap Verdict

They agree precisely on the one thing that is weighed, and diverge on everything they estimate. Across five pairings the weight gap averages +0.24 kg with a standard deviation of 0.35, and on 21 May 2025 both read 79.5 kg exactly. The body-fat gap runs from −4.2 to +3.7 pp — but three of those five scans were run against a height that was 3 to 7 cm wrong, so most of that range is a keyboard, not a sensor.

Neither device is “right”. Bioimpedance passes a small current through the body, measures opposition to it, and infers composition through a regression fitted on some reference population. The two vendors fit different regressions on different populations through different electrode paths — the InBody uses hand and foot electrodes, which is why it can resolve arms and legs and the foot-only scale cannot, and why only it can report a phase angle. Neither number is “your body fat percentage”; each is one estimator's opinion, and one of them was being asked the wrong question.

What the trend says once you stop comparing devices

One device, one estimator, 142 scans, 26 months. Absolute values stay uncertain; direction and magnitude of change do not.

The record has a hole in it, and the hole is the injury

Nothing was measured for 97 days — 21 Oct 2025 to 27 Jan 2026 — and that window contains the MRI on 10 Nov 2025 and the six weeks of fortnightly re-injury that followed. The scale did not record a decline; it recorded nothing, and then recorded the result. A chart must draw that as a break, never as a line between the two endpoints, because the interpolation would invent a smooth gain that almost certainly was not smooth.

Since Stage 2A began on 20 July

Three scans: 81.8 → 81.9 → 82.2 kg. Against the 17 July reading of 80.8 kg that is +1.4 kg, which the model splits +0.7 kg fat and +0.7 kg fat-free. Do not read that split. Both halves come from the same two numbers, so it is one observation wearing two hats, and the 0.6 pp body-fat move behind it sits between the 0.23 pp mean and the 0.80 pp maximum of pure measurement noise. The correct statement is that weight is up 1.4 kg and the composition has not yet moved detectably.

That is also the physiologically expected answer. Two weeks into the first loaded block after months of rehab, a kilogram or so arrives as muscle glycogen and the water bound to it — roughly three grams of water per gram of glycogen — not as tissue.

The real arc

Leanest recorded was 9 Jun 2025 — 77.6 kg at 15.5%, twelve kilograms of fat. Heaviest was the first scan ever, 7 Jun 2024 — 85.9 kg at 19.2%. Today sits at 82.2 kg and 17.6%, roughly two thirds of the way back up from the 2025 low. Over the full 786 days the net is −3.7 kg of weight, −2.0 kg of fat and −1.7 kg of fat-free mass — losing lean tissue alongside fat is what a year interrupted by back injury looks like, and it is the single most useful sentence this dataset produces.
